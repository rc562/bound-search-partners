Six-dollar diesel is a leadership problem.

Diesel hit a record on September 11. Every manufacturer pays the same price, so the price won't decide who has a good year. The people running your plants will.

The sixth in a series of Bound Search Partners briefings on the 2026 supply chain crisis and what it means for the people running U.S. manufacturing.

Diesel just set a record.

On September 11, AAA's national average for diesel crossed $6 a gallon for the first time. Today it is $6.27. A year ago it was $3.69. The old record, $5.82 from June 2022, stood for four years and fell on September 4. California is above $8.

The reason is the Strait of Hormuz. It has been effectively closed for six months, and it normally carries roughly a fifth of the world's oil and refined fuel. On September 9, new U.S. and Iranian strikes pushed Brent crude back over $100. It has held above $100 since, mostly between $104 and $109.

Factories are still busy in spite of it. The August ISM index was 54.6, the eighth straight month of growth, and production came in at 58.3. That is the strongest run since 2022. The one soft spot: new orders fell three points. Worth watching.

This is a refining problem, not an oil problem.

This is not really an oil shock. Crude is still well below its 2022 peak. Diesel is setting records. The gap between what a barrel of crude costs and what a barrel of diesel sells for (refiners call it the crack spread) passed $100 on September 1. Before the war it was around $20. In plain terms: the world is short refineries, not short oil.

That is the harder version of the problem. The Strategic Petroleum Reserve holds crude, not diesel, so a release does not help much. Nobody builds a refinery in a quarter. Goldman Sachs expects U.S. diesel refining margins to stay near $63 a barrel into 2027. Every official forecast that had oil under $80 by now has already been overtaken.

One more thing. Most companies do not appear to have passed the diesel increase on to customers yet; the first hard read comes on third-quarter earnings calls. If that holds, the cost is coming straight out of plant margin right now. The pricing fights with customers have not started. They will, and they will be won or lost by the people in the room, not by anything Washington does.

Your competitors pay the same price you do.

Diesel is a commodity. Same price, same tariffs, same carrier surcharges, for everyone. So when two similar plants end the year with different margins, the difference will be who ran them.

Here is what concerns me. Diesel was last at this level in the summer of 2022. A lot of the plant managers, supply chain VPs and logistics directors in those seats today were not the ones holding the P&L back then. Many were promoted or hired in 2023 and 2024, when freight was cheap and orders were steady. They are good at their jobs. They have just never run a plant with diesel above five dollars. The question is not when they were hired. It is what they were responsible for the last time this happened.

Every plant is paying the same price for diesel. Very few have a leader who has run one through prices like this before.

The people who ran operations through 2022 do a few specific things, and they do them early. They reprice freight-heavy contracts within weeks instead of waiting for renewal. They consolidate loads and switch shipping modes before the surcharge letters arrive. They know which products and which customers stop making money at $6, and they say so. They have cut a route before, so cutting another one does not paralyze them. You do not learn that from a dashboard. You learn it by doing it wrong once, then right.

Heavy freight, thin margins, and January 1.

Anyone shipping heavy product on thin margins: food and beverage, building products, packaging, agricultural and construction equipment. Anything palletized and trucked regionally. Daimler Truck said last week that diesel prices are delaying fleet renewal. Fleets will run older trucks longer. That means more maintenance and more downtime, and both land on plant leadership.

Then January 1, 2027. As announced, new tariffs on Canadian auto parts take effect and the tariff on Canadian vehicles doubles to 50 percent. If your bill of materials crosses that border, the freight shock and the tariff shock arrive in the same quarter, on the same desk.

And underneath all of it, the hiring market is already tight. There is $2.07 trillion in announced U.S. manufacturing investment across 237 companies, and more than 550,000 open manufacturing jobs, up roughly 150,000 in a year and back to late-2023 levels. New plants are recruiting from existing ones. They want the exact people described above, and they will pay for 2022 experience whether the job posting says so or not.

Things we're keeping an eye on.

Oct 1The September ISM report. If new orders keep falling while production holds, the cycle is turning. If not, August was noise.
Oct–NovThird-quarter earnings calls. The first time industrial companies have to say whether they absorbed the diesel cost or passed it on, and how customers responded.
Jan 2027Canadian tariffs. Do the parts tariffs take effect as announced, and does the 50 percent on vehicles follow?
WeeklyFreight-to-margin, not the freight bill. Margin by lane and by customer at today's rate. First-quarter contracts are being priced now. The person who owns that number should know it without opening a spreadsheet.
Q4Search timelines. An operating leader with real 2022 experience is already a sixty-plus-day search, and new plants are working the same short list. A seat opened in October fills in the new year. One opened in January is a Q1 story.
LiveThe crack spread, not the crude price. A ceasefire lowers crude. It does not build refineries or restore Russian fuel exports. Diesel comes down when refining margins do, and Goldman has those elevated into 2027.
AlwaysWho ran what in 2022. For each plant, supply chain and logistics leader: what were they responsible for in June 2022, when diesel was $5.82? If nobody on the top team was running a P&L then, that is the exposure. It will not show up on a balance sheet.

What decides it.

A lot of companies will spend the next two quarters on hedging, surcharge language and whether to hold price. Fair questions. But every competitor is having the same meeting, with the same information, at the same price per gallon. Nobody gets ahead in that room.

You get ahead with the plant manager who repriced in 2022 and remembers what broke. The supply chain lead who has already cut a route. The logistics director who understands this is a refining problem and is not waiting for a reserve release. Those people exist. There are fewer of them than there are plants, and the new plants have already figured that out.

So the question this quarter is not what diesel does next. It is whether your people have done this before. And if they have not, how much you are willing to pay to find out.

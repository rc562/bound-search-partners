Cyber readiness is everyone's job now.

Over the past year and a half, the cyberattacks that shut down plants and distribution centers rarely started inside the company that was hit. They came in through a customer, a distributor, or a software vendor. That means readiness can't sit with IT alone. The CFO decides whether to pay suppliers early. The procurement lead knows which outside systems the shipping dock depends on. The plant manager may have to run shipping on paper for a week. IT has one seat at that table, and this briefing is about the others.

A small supplier's experience, one year on.

A year ago this month, Jaguar Land Rover was about a week into restarting its UK plants after six weeks of nothing. An attacker had gotten into JLR's systems around September 1, 2025. JLR shut its own systems down to contain it, which stopped the attack and also stopped the plants. Three factories that build about a thousand vehicles a day went quiet, the workers went home, and the first engines didn't run again until October 8. The lines came back in phases after that.

The story I keep telling is smaller than that. About a month into the shutdown, a BBC reporter went and talked to the suppliers. One of them was Genex UK, an 18-person supplier that presses metal parts for JLR. Nothing had happened to Genex's computers. Genex's orders from JLR had simply stopped. With the orders went the invoices, and with the invoices went the cash. The owner told the reporter he'd had to let some of his people go. Evtec, a tier-one supplier with about 1,250 employees, said its plants were at a virtual standstill. Nobody had attacked either company. The money had just stopped coming down the chain.

I've told that story to a handful of CEOs since, because it's the clearest picture I've found of what this risk actually is. Your own plant is usually fine. The trouble starts when a customer or a vendor goes down, and you feel it first in receivables or at the shipping dock.

What supply chain leaders are observing.

What surprised me when I went looking for numbers was who's worried. KPMG's 2026 US Supply Chain Survey asked 462 supply chain leaders at U.S. companies with at least a billion dollars in revenue what keeps them up, and cyber security came out as the top supply chain risk, ahead of deeper-tier supplier risk in second place. These are people who run supply chains for a living. Asked about their own supply chains, they ranked cyber risk above the problems they manage every day. Seventy-seven percent of the same group said they have a talent gap in procurement and supply chain, which matters later in this piece.

The trend line is the reason to have this conversation this year. In its 2025 edition, Verizon's Data Breach Investigations Report found a third party involved in 30 percent of the breaches it studied, double the share from the year before. The 2026 edition, published in May, shows third-party and supply chain breaches up another 60 percent, to nearly half of all the breaches it examined. Verizon's own explanation for the speed is AI. Attackers are using AI to find and exploit software flaws in hours instead of months. Microsoft's Digital Defense Report, out October 1, shows the same pattern in its own data, with the attack timeline shrinking from days to minutes. The tools are getting easier to use for defenders and attackers alike, and attackers don't have a budget cycle.

Two caveats. First, the KPMG respondents run billion-dollar companies with real security budgets, and most of the manufacturers I work with are a fraction of that size. My opinion, and only mine: the smaller companies are less ready, not more, because the same handful of people are already running everything and nobody has a spare afternoon for a drill. Second, the survey sample is American, while the central story in this piece is British. I'm using the JLR story anyway, because the mechanics are the same in Michigan as in the Midlands. And UNFI, which comes up in a minute, is about as American as a grocery truck.

The CEO and the board: naming the risk.

Go back to JLR for a second and look at the size of it. JLR went six weeks without production. It booked around £196 million in cyber-related costs in a single quarter, and wholesale volumes fell 24 percent. The strain on its supply base was severe enough that the UK government stepped in with a £1.5 billion loan guarantee so the smaller suppliers would still exist on restart day. That is a board-level event by any definition, and my guess is that in most companies the plan for it still lives two floors down, with the head of IT.

The question I'd put to any leadership team is who owns the plan for a key supplier or distributor going dark. My guess at the usual answer: a pause, then everyone looks at the IT lead. The IT lead owns the backups and the firewalls, and should. The IT lead does not decide whether to pay a supplier early, and does not run the plant. So the CEO's job is to name an owner for the rest of the plan, the way someone owns safety, and to make sure that person has actually sat down with the controller and the plant manager. The board's role is smaller: ask for that name once a quarter, and ask what's changed since the last review.

A question for the CEO

Who on this team owns the plan for a supplier or distributor going dark?

The CFO: liquidity when operations stop.

The money is where JLR's problem became everyone else's. JLR's standard terms were sixty days after invoice. A supplier with nothing shipping can't wait sixty days for its last check when no new invoices are being paid, so JLR improvised in public: manual payments first, then a dedicated supplier help desk, then on October 7, 2025, a financing program to pay suppliers cash up front. Each of those was a treasury decision, made within weeks and under pressure, by people who had probably never been asked to make one.

United Natural Foods is the American version. UNFI is the main distributor for Whole Foods. On June 5, 2025, UNFI found an intruder in its systems and deliberately took those systems offline. As a result, it couldn't take or fill orders the normal way. Shelves went bare in places. UNFI later put the damage at $350 to $400 million in lost net sales before insurance, and about $20 million of that was just the cost of the manual workarounds UNFI ran while its systems were down. That's twenty million dollars spent on people doing by hand what the system used to do.

That's the CFO's side of it, and it's concrete. Who has the authority to pay a supplier early, and how fast can that decision get made on a Tuesday afternoon? What does the insurance actually cover once the claim goes in, and does a supplier's outage count at all? And the one almost nobody has asked: how much cash runway are the small suppliers you can't ship without carrying right now?

A question for the CFO

Which of our suppliers would be out of cash in six weeks, and have we ever asked them?

Procurement: understanding true dependencies.

The second-ranked risk in the KPMG survey was suppliers deeper in the chain, and in practice it's the same risk as the first. The company that gets hit might sit two tiers below you: a plating shop that your tier-one supplier uses and you've never heard of. Or the weak point might be your freight broker's booking software or the warehouse system at your 3PL. Every manufacturer has a bill of materials for every product. Far fewer keep a comparable list of the outside systems their shipping dock depends on. I'd bet that list takes an afternoon to build and surprises the people building it.

There's a procurement angle on the vendor side too. CISA, the federal cyber agency, published guidance in January 2025 for anyone buying operational technology, the controllers and equipment that actually run a plant. The gist was to make security part of the purchase: ask how the vendor handles updates and who can get into the system remotely, and write the answers into the contract. In operating terms, those are the same questions you already ask about spare parts and service response, pointed at a different part of the machine. The buyer owns that conversation, and the buyer is usually procurement.

A question for the COO

Which three outside companies could stop our shipping by going dark tomorrow?

The plant: planning to operate offline.

UNFI ran on workarounds for about three weeks before its core systems came back. JLR restarted in phases, engines first, then the rest. In both cases somebody on the floor had to work out how to receive material, build product, pick orders, and ship them with the screens dark, and those people had to work it out that same morning, with whatever was already on the dock.

That piece belongs to the plant manager, and most of it is planning. How do we release work orders and hold quality records on paper for a week, then get them back into the system afterward? Who signs off on shipping a lot without the usual scan? The people who can answer those questions are already in the building, and in my experience the good ones have already thought about it. What's rarer is a plant that has actually tested the plan. I'd want to see the plant run on paper once, on a slow shift, before it has to be done for real.

A question for plant leadership

Has anyone here run a shift without the system, on purpose, to see what breaks?

IT and HR: defense, and the people behind it.

None of this takes anything away from IT. Backups and the plan to shut systems down fast and bring them back clean are technical work, and IT owns them. UNFI took its own systems offline on June 5, and that decision belongs to exactly those people. What IT can't do is write the payroll plan at Genex or the pick-ticket plan in UNFI's warehouses, and those turned out to be the plans that mattered most to the people on the floor.

HR's piece is smaller and it's about the bench. KPMG's 77 percent talent gap describes the same thin group of people who'd be running the plant on paper that week. A supervisor who can run a shift without the system is worth knowing about before the week you need her. Knowing who can do that is a talent question, and it belongs on the HR agenda next to succession.

Returning to the small supplier.

Bound Search Partners is a retained search firm, and most of the plant and supply chain leaders I talk to have thought about this more than their companies have asked them to. It's become one of the questions I ask in a search: walk me through the first week your biggest supplier goes dark. The good answers are specific. The best candidates name the supplier, and they've already had the conversation with the controller. I read every note personally, at bob@boundsearch.com.

I keep coming back to the owner of that small supplier. He ran his business right, and someone else's problem landed on his payroll within a few weeks. Somewhere in your supply chain is a company that depends on you the same way, and a vendor you depend on just as much. I'd want to know who's on the hook for that before the call comes.

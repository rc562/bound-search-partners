Yes, nobody's quitting. Don't call it loyalty.

Nobody's quitting right now, and a lot of companies are reading that as loyalty. I don't buy it. Some of those people want to be there. Some just don't like their odds out there right now. From the dashboard, you can't tell which.

I talk to people on both sides of this every week, and honestly, plenty of the ones sitting tight aren't thrilled. They just don't like their odds out there right now. You've probably done the same math on your own job this year.

Your attrition number is only telling you half the story.

With quits at 1.9%, the exit door is mostly shut, so the attrition dashboard looks great. The trouble is it can't tell the difference between someone who wants to stay and someone who's afraid to leave, and nobody explains that in an exit interview, because nobody's exiting.

I talk to a lot of CEOs who are proud of their turnover number right now, and I get it. But when I ask which of their people are quietly taking recruiter calls, most of them don't know.

The early signs are a lot quieter than a resignation letter. You get fewer ideas in the Monday meeting, and good people who do the job well and not one thing more.

Ask yourself: which three people would I be most afraid to lose once the market opens up? Do they know that?

Talk to your top operators before a recruiter does.

What I'd do is sit down with your top 10 to 20 operators, from the plant managers to the engineers you can't replace, and ask them what would make them leave and what they'd change. HR calls it a stay conversation, which is fine. The point is you hear it before a recruiter does.

The part people skip is fixing something afterward. It doesn't have to be big. It might be the capex request that's been sitting since spring, or the supervisor they've been short all year. What matters is that they see you heard them, and quickly, because listening and then filing it away tells them exactly how much the answer mattered.

Ask your plant manager: what's the one thing that would get you to take a recruiter's call?

Know what the hiring freeze is actually costing you.

I understand why so many companies froze hiring. The negative comments in ISM's August report keep coming back to pricing volatility, lead times, the Iran war and tariffs, and one manufacturer told ISM, "The economy is annoying; it is getting in the way of otherwise good business." Most CEOs I talk to would sign it.

The freeze feels like the safe call, but somebody's paying for it. Usually it's the person covering the gap, like the plant manager who's been running two sites since spring, or the ops leader who never got a number two and is now doing both jobs. Those people are tired, and they're exactly who a recruiter calls first.

And the other side isn't standing still. Challenger, Gray & Christmas says 46% of this year's announced hiring plans came from manufacturing, and Andy Challenger adds that "it doesn't appear those positions are being filled quickly." So the competition for good operators has already started, even if it doesn't feel that way from inside your plant.

At your next staff meeting: which open seat costs us more to leave empty than to fill?

Build the bench while it's quiet.

Hiring will pick back up at some point. Nobody knows the month, and the thing is, everyone will go after the same leaders at the same time.

So I'd map your critical seats now. For each one, write down who's ready today and who'll be ready in 6 to 12 months. The blank lines tell you where to start.

The quiet works for you, too. In my experience, strong operators who aren't looking are often open to the right conversation in a market like this, and that goes for your competitors' people as much as yours.

Ask your leadership team: if our plant manager gave notice Monday, who runs the plant Tuesday?

We spend most of our week talking with manufacturing leaders who aren't looking for a job, and that's where you hear the difference between staying and being stuck. Want a second read on your bench? I read every note myself: bob@boundsearch.com.

Things are quiet right now. Me, I'd spend the quiet talking to my best people.
